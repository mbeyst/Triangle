WHAT YOU NEED

Three people. Paper and pen. A decision to make.

That's it.

---

THE FIVE STEPS

1. SURFACE THE DECISION

What needs deciding? Not what do we need to discuss — what do we need to decide?

Write it as one sentence. If it has "and" in it, split it into two decisions.

Example: "Do we approve the budget for the new project?" — not "Let's talk about the budget."

The goal: Frame it. Vote. Record. Move.


2. CHOOSE A FACILITATOR

The facilitator structures the agenda, frames the conditions, and closes with a review. They do not chair the meeting — they design it. The facilitator is also a voter.

The person who surfaced the decision usually facilitates.

If someone else is better suited to structure this particular decision, that person facilitates instead.

If the group cannot agree, the authorised person selects.

---

3. FORM THE TRIAD

Ask the group: who are the best three people for this decision?

Anyone who wants to be in it volunteers. Anyone willing to take part is eligible.

- Exactly three? You're set.
- More than three and divisible by three (six, nine, twelve)? Form that many triads — each takes one decision.
- Not divisible by three? Draw lots for the remaining spots. Write names on paper, pull from a hat. This is not random. It is surrendered. "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord" (Proverbs 16:33).
- No one volunteers? The authorised person picks three. Use this only when the group can't nominate — the community's knowledge is the best signal you have.

Summoning an outsider

The triad may summon an outsider to speak to a condition. The outsider gives input, not a vote. The triad then decides as normal. The summoning is recorded: who was summoned, why, and what they said.

The recommendation is to keep your decisions in the three.


4. FRAME THE CONDITIONS

Before anyone votes, the facilitator names the conditions the decision rests on.

"What has to be true for this to be the right call?"

Write each condition as a yes/no question. Keep it to two or three. If there are more, the decision needs splitting.

Example: "Do we approve the budget?" rests on —
  Condition 1: Is the cost within our means?
  Condition 2: Does the project serve our purpose?
  Condition 3: Can we reverse this if it goes wrong?

The triad can challenge the conditions before voting. If someone says the conditions are wrong, reframe them. The conditions are not imposed — they are stated by a person who is also a voter.

You can also mark a condition neutral — you don't have a position on it. A neutral vote does not count toward the two-of-three. If fewer than two people take a position on a condition, that condition is unresolved and the decision cannot pass on it.


5. VOTE — TWO OF THREE

Each person votes on each condition. Yes, no, or neutral.

Two out of three yes votes on any condition means it passes. One out of three means it fails.

A neutral vote is set aside. It neither helps nor blocks. But if only one person votes yes and the other two are neutral, the condition is unresolved — the decision cannot pass on it.

If all three agree, record it as unanimous. That is the strongest signal. But it is not required.

The dissent is recorded. Who said no, and why. "By the mouth of two or three witnesses every charge may be established" (Matthew 18:16).

Write it down:

  Decision: [one sentence]
  Conditions: [list]
  Vote per condition: [who voted what — yes, no, or neutral]
  Result: [approved / not approved / unresolved]
  Dissent: [who dissented, and why]
  Expected outcome: [what we expect to happen if we're right]
  Date: [today]
  Triad: [three names]


6. LEAVE THE WINDOW OPEN

The amendment window stays open after the meeting.

Each member of the triad can change their mind on something they said during the meeting. They must say what they would change about their own position and why. Not just "I disagree" — a reasoned case.

The closure period is determined by the Group. In many cases it may be a matter of minutes after the meeting. After that, the window closes and the decision stands — unless an outcome proves it wrong.


---

TRACK IT OVER TIME

At the next meeting, check where you are with last week's outcomes. Any triad member can mark an outcome as complete.

If there is disagreement, that can be recorded as in the normal procedure. Dissent does not require a reason, although it is strongly recommended.

This is the competence signal. Over time, you see who has been right, on what, and how often. That tells you who to route decisions to next time.

"The heart of the wise seeks knowledge" (Proverbs 18:15). Wisdom is accumulated, tested, and refined through what comes to pass.


---

IF IT GOES WRONG

You approved something that failed — false acceptance.
  Flag it. Name what was wrong. Reopen the decision.
  Send it back to the triad that decided it. They cannot be overruled — but they can be asked to reconsider.

You blocked something that would have worked — false rejection.
  This is harder. Someone has to say "we got it wrong by saying no."
  Give it procedural weight. Let them make the case. Let the triad reopen.
  "Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in your power to do it" (Proverbs 3:27).

Either way: the original decision stays in the record. It is not erased. It is marked as revisited, with the reasoning and the correction. That is accountability — not the absence of failure, but the presence of a path back.

"The way of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day" (Proverbs 4:18). The light comes from each step being recorded, examined, and corrected.


---

FOR THE FACILITATOR

You do not chair the meeting. You design it.

Before the meeting:
  - List the decisions, not the topics.
  - Decompose each decision into its conditions.
  - Identify who should be in the room for each.
  - Sequence by dependency — if B depends on A, A goes first.

During the meeting:
  - Frame each decision and its conditions.
  - Let the triad challenge the framing before voting.
  - Record the vote, the dissent, and the expected outcome.
  - Ask: is there a voice we have not heard?

After the meeting:
  - Keep the amendment window open.
  - Track the outcome.
  - Feed what you learned into the next meeting.

The meeting is not the container. The decision is the container. The meeting is the shape the decision takes when the right people are brought together for it.


---

ONE-PAGE SUMMARY

  1. Surface the decision — one sentence, yes or no.
  2. Choose a facilitator — usually the person who surfaced it; if not, someone better suited; if the group can't agree, the authorised person selects.
  3. Form the triad — nominate from the group, draw lots if needed.
  4. Frame the conditions — what has to be true? Two or three, yes/no each. Mark neutral if you have no position.
  5. Vote — two of three. Record the dissent. Neutral votes are set aside.
  6. Leave the window open — anyone can flag a bad decision, with reasons.

  Track outcomes. Route to the competent. Correct what fails.

  That's it. Paper and pen. Three people. One decision at a time.

ADVANCED OPTIONS

These are for when the simple path doesn't resolve. Most meetings won't need them.

---

FACILITATOR CANNOT BE AGREED

If the group cannot settle on a facilitator by discussion:

1. Vote. Each person names one facilitator. You cannot vote for yourself.
2. The person with the most votes facilitates.
3. If there is a tie, draw lots. Write the tied names on paper, pull from a hat. "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord" (Proverbs 16:33).

This is the same pattern as forming the triad: the community's knowledge of who is best suited is the signal. Voting surfaces it. Lots resolve what the community cannot.

---

NEUTRAL VOTES — DETAIL

A neutral vote means: I do not have a position on this condition.

It is not "maybe." It is not "I abstain." It is: this condition is not one I can judge on the information I have, or it is not relevant to my part in this decision.

Rules:
- A neutral vote does not count toward the two-of-three.
- A condition needs at least two non-neutral votes to be resolved.
- If a condition has fewer than two non-neutral votes, it is unresolved. The decision cannot pass on it. The facilitator should ask whether the condition needs reframing, more information, or a different triad.

Why this exists: sometimes a condition genuinely does not apply to one member of the triad — they lack the knowledge, or it falls outside their part of the decision. Forcing a yes/no produces noise. Neutral produces a signal: this condition needs attention.

It does not create a third outcome. The decision still passes or fails on each condition. Neutral just means the condition is not yet settled.


---

FOR MULTIPLE DECISIONS

This guide covers one decision with one triad.

When a meeting has several decisions, the agenda is not a list — it is a routing problem. Different decisions may need different triads. The facilitator decomposes the agenda, routes each decision to the right triad, and closes with a review of the whole.
