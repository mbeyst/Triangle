About this document

Triangle is a decision-making process organised around triads. This document describes each process, what it does, and its grounding in Scripture.

The processes are: forming the triad, choosing a facilitator, framing the agenda and conditions, voting, recording and aggregation, scaling through decomposition, tracking outcomes, the amendment window, and the role of the system.

The document follows the lifecycle of a Triangle meeting. Scripture is cited inline by reference; the full text of each passage is given at the end.

Limitations and project status are documented separately.

—

1 The triad

A triad is three people making one decision. The decision is recorded per condition, with each person voting yes, no, or neutral.

The competence condition

The triad assumes three people can make better decisions than one. It does not claim to make people competent. The question the design sets out to answer is whether an organisation can tell, for any given decision, whether that assumption holds.

Scripture affirms the principle and names the condition: counsel multiplies strength, but only when the counselors carry understanding (Proverbs 11:14; 15:22; 24:6). The proverb does not promise that numbers alone save — it promises that wise counsel does.

Two are better than one, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken (Ecclesiastes 4:9–12). The cord is threefold not by formula but by the nature of things — strength in plurality, provided the strands hold.

The Apocrypha sharpens the principle: seek counsel before a matter, and turn not aside (Sirach 37:5–6). Wisdom is the voice of the Lord (Wisdom of Solomon 6:24). Counsel is not a numbers game; it is a search for the voice that speaks wisdom.

Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labor in vain (Psalm 127:1). The triad is the house. It is built by people, but it stands only by God's design.

The limit of the principle is that it depends on the character of the counsel. Trust in the Lord with all your heart and do not lean on your own understanding (Proverbs 3:5–6). The counsel is only as sound as the orientation of those who give it. The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to advice (Proverbs 12:15). Without the disposition to hear, plurality becomes noise.

Independence fails in organisations. Two are better than one — but the threefold cord is broken when the strands are not truly independent. Shared managers, shared briefings, shared incentives: once those who counsel are bound by the same interests, the cord is of one strand, and it is not quickly broken because it was never three.

The triad captures most of the available gain before the assumptions underwriting it break down, at a fraction of the cost. Triangle's outcome-tracking is built to surface over time whether the assumption held — recording what was decided, what it rested on, and how it turned out — so that competence becomes visible rather than assumed.

2 The facilitator

The facilitator structures the agenda, frames the conditions, and closes with a review. They do not chair the meeting — they design it. The facilitator is also a voter.

Choosing a facilitator

The person who surfaced the decision usually facilitates.

If someone else is better suited to structure this particular decision, that person facilitates instead.

If the group cannot agree, the authorised person selects.

The democratic bridge

The mechanism: the group is asked who should facilitate. The community's knowledge of who is best suited is the signal. The selection is the accountability.

This is the same pattern as forming the triad: the community identifies the qualified, the authority confirms, and the competence signal begins.

The people chose, and Moses appointed (Deuteronomy 1:13–15). The community picked, and the apostles appointed (Acts 6:3). The whole church was involved in the selection (Acts 15:22). The people's recognition of who the elders were was the signal (Numbers 11:16). The churches chose their own representative (2 Corinthians 8:19).

The fast mode

The authorised person simply selects the facilitator. No nomination. No group input.

This is the fallback, not the default. It fits when the authority has better knowledge than the group, when speed matters more than participation, or when the decision is sufficiently specialised that only the authority knows who is competent. But it bypasses the community's knowledge, and that knowledge is the specific asset the process needs.

Jesus called to him those whom he desired, and they came to him (Mark 3:13–14). God told Moses to lay his hand on Joshua, a man in whom is the Spirit (Numbers 27:18–21).

The voice that was not chosen

God told Samuel to anoint one of Jesse's sons as king. The elder sons were brought in. Each looked the part. Samuel assumed the eldest was the one. God said no: the Lord sees not as man sees. Man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart (1 Samuel 16). David was the youngest. He was not invited to the meeting. He was out keeping the sheep. He was not nominated. God chose him anyway.

The democratic bridge captures the community's knowledge. But the community's knowledge is not God's knowledge. The community can nominate the obvious choice and miss the right one. David was the right one, and he was not nominated.

This is what the amendment window is for. The amendment window is the standing invitation for the David — the person who should have been in the triad but was not nominated. The democratic bridge is the cold start. The amendment window is the correction. Together they form the complete pattern: the community nominates, the authority confirms, and the structure makes room for the voice that was missed.

Micaiah was not on the agenda. He was summoned because a dissenting voice was needed (1 Kings 22). The democratic bridge forms the triad. The amendment window is the mechanism that says: is there a Micaiah we have not heard?

3 The agenda and conditions

The agenda is not a list of topics. It is a list of decisions. Each decision is decomposed into the conditions it rests on, and each condition is routed to the triad best placed to judge it.

The agenda as a routing problem

If Triangle routes different decisions to different triads based on competence, then a single agenda with five items might need five different triads. The traditional model — everyone in one room, go through items A to E — does not work. You need a different kind of meeting.

The problem is not the triad. The problem is the agenda.

Scripture does not show one model of meetings. It shows several, and the pattern is consistent: the structure follows the question, not the other way round.

Moses sat from morning to evening, judging every case himself. Jethro said: decompose. Select capable men. Let them judge the small matters. Let only the hard cases come to you (Exodus 18). The agenda was not flattened — it was decomposed. The meeting structure followed the work, not the other way round.

One question: do Gentiles need circumcision? The agenda was set by the question. But the process was not one debate. Paul and Barnabas reported what God had done. Peter spoke from his experience. James spoke from Scripture and gave the ruling. Different people spoke at different points. Not everyone spoke on everything (Acts 15).

The apostles said: it is not right that we should give up preaching the word of God to serve tables. They delegated a specific task to specific people chosen for that task (Acts 6).

The court prophets all said go up, and the king's agenda was set. One voice said no. Micaiah was not on the agenda. He was summoned because a dissenting voice was needed (1 Kings 22).

The people gathered as one. But the Levites went among them, helping the people understand, each in their own group (Nehemiah 8).

When you come together, each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation. Let all things be done for building up (1 Corinthians 14:26). The meeting is not one person's agenda. It is structured participation.

Framing conditions

Before anyone votes, the facilitator names the conditions the decision rests on.

"What has to be true for this to be the right call?"

Each condition is written as a yes/no question. Keep it to two or three. If there are more, the decision needs splitting.

The triad can challenge the conditions before voting. If someone says the conditions are wrong, the facilitator reframes. The conditions are not imposed by the system — they are stated by a human who is also a voter, and can be outvoted on the outcome.

Summoning an outsider

The triad may summon an outsider to speak to a condition. The outsider gives input, not a vote. The triad then decides as normal. The summoning is recorded: who was summoned, why, and what they said.

The recommendation is to keep your decisions in the three.

A condition can be marked neutral — the facilitator does not have a position on it. A neutral vote does not count toward the two-of-three. If fewer than two people take a position on a condition, it is unresolved, and the decision cannot pass on it.

Writing the agenda

Surface the decisions. What needs deciding? Not what do we need to discuss — what do we need to decide?

Decompose each decision into its conditions.

Identify who needs to be in the room for each decision — not who should be informed, but who should be deciding.

Sequence by dependency — if B depends on A, A comes first. If A and C are independent, they can happen in parallel.

Leave room for the voice that was not planned. The amendment window is the standing invitation for the dissenting voice. But the agenda itself should include a standing item: is there a voice we have not heard?

Close with a review of the whole. Every meeting that spawns sub-decisions should end with a closing decision that reviews the whole — a decision: does the whole hold, given what we decided in the parts?

4 Voting — two of three

Each person votes on each condition: yes, no, or neutral.

Two out of three yes votes on any resolved condition means it passes. Zero yes votes means it fails.

Neutral

A neutral vote means "I do not have a position on this condition." It is not maybe, and it is not abstention. It is an honest signal that the condition is not answerable by this person — either they lack the knowledge, or it falls outside their part of the decision.

A neutral vote is set aside. It neither helps nor blocks. A condition needs at least two non-neutral votes to be resolved. If a condition has fewer than two non-neutral votes, it is unresolved, and the decision cannot pass on it. The facilitator should ask whether the condition needs reframing, more information, or a different triad.

The binary rule still governs: two yes votes are required to pass. Neutral does not create a third outcome. It creates a "not yet settled" state that blocks the decision from passing on that condition. That is consistent with the witness principle — a condition must actually be decided, not assumed.

Why two of three

Scripture is plain that justice must be administered without partiality. You shall not be partial in judgment; you shall hear the small and the great alike (Deuteronomy 19:17; 2 Chronicles 19:7). The rule must treat every voice the same and both sides of a question the same.

This is the biblical grounding for two of three. It is not an assumption or a convention. It is the pattern Scripture establishes for establishing a matter: on the evidence of two or three witnesses a matter is established (Numbers 35:30; Matthew 18:16; 2 Corinthians 13:1).

Two witnesses establish; three confirm. The binary rule — two of three — is the minimal structure that satisfies the scriptural requirement and no more. It gives every voice equal weight, treats both sides symmetrically, and responds when a mind changes: if the one who dissented is persuaded, the matter is established.

It also tells you what is wrong with the alternatives. A supermajority threshold — requiring two-thirds or three-quarters to decide — violates the principle of equal treatment by privileging the status quo. The rule makes it harder to change a decision than to leave it where it is, which means the option that was there first has an advantage the other does not. Unanimity does the same thing more extremely: a single dissenter blocks the decision, and the status quo prevails by default.

This is why unanimity is preferred in Triangle but not required. It is the strongest possible consensus signal — everyone agrees — and when it appears it is worth recording. But as a decision rule it is paralysing: one person can hold the whole group. The design prefers the outcome that moves, and records the dissent, to the outcome that does not move because one person objected.

For questions with more than two options, Scripture does not prescribe a single aggregation rule, but it does instruct that matters be set before the whole and that counsel be sought from many. In the multitude of people is the king's honour, but in the want of people is the destruction of the prince (Proverbs 14:28). Triangle decomposes multi-option questions into a series of binary ones rather than asking a triad to rank alternatives, so that each binary choice can be judged by the witness principle.

5 Recording and aggregation

Positions are recorded per condition rather than on the decision as a whole. The record shows what each person voted on each condition, what the result was, and what was expected to follow.

Why per condition

Where a decision rests on multiple conditions, aggregating over the conditions and aggregating over the conclusion can yield opposite results from the same set of individual judgments. Three judges, each internally consistent, each voting on two premises and a conclusion, can produce a majority for each premise and a majority against the conclusion.

Scripture recognises this danger. There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death (Proverbs 14:12). A conclusion can appear sound while the conditions beneath it are unsound, and the soundness of the parts does not justify the whole. The wise man examines the premises, not only the conclusion.

Blessed is the man who meditates on wisdom, and who reasons with understanding (Tobit 4:18–19). The warning is not against reasoning — it is against reasoning badly, on conditions that have not been examined. The decision rests on what it rests on, and the record must show what that is.

Recording per condition corrects a measurable bias: because concluding "yes" on a conjunctive decision requires being right about every condition, conclusion-level voting rejects sound multi-condition proposals at a higher rate than the evidence warrants. Recording per condition reduces that bias, at the cost of being more permissive on decisions that should be blocked.

Which error is cheaper depends on reversibility. We treat it as a parameter rather than pretending there is a neutral default.

Neutral votes are informative in themselves. A pattern of neutrals on a condition tells the organisation that the triad lacks the knowledge to judge it — which is a signal about who should be in the next triad, or whether the condition needs to be decomposed further. Recording neutrals is recording something real, not filling silence with a guess.

Group agency

For a group to bear responsibility — for "the organisation decided" to be coherent — its judgments must be consistent and closed under entailment. If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand (Mark 3:25). Consistency is not a natural property of people in a room; it must be engineered. That is the claim underlying the interest in institutional accountability, and it is a design problem rather than a gift of assembly.

Consult the Lord, for their works are his (1 Maccabees 3:18–22). The pattern is not merely human deliberation — it is deliberation that acknowledges a higher court. The organisation's record is its own accountability; the final judgment is not ours to claim.

6 Scaling through decomposition

Triangle does not add people to decisions or aggregate verdicts across triads. It decomposes decisions.

Each decision is a node. Edges record dependency: premise, constraint, or delegation. The structure is a directed acyclic graph.

A triad ruling on a large question spawns the sub-decisions it does not own, each routed to its own triad, in parallel. A triad blocked for want of an input spawns the blocking question and routes it laterally to whoever owns it, rather than escalating to a less-informed authority.

Scripture testifies to this pattern of delegation. When Jethro saw Moses judging all the people from morning until evening, he said: this thing is too hard for you. You cannot alone bear it. Select capable men from all the land, men who fear God, who are trustworthy and hate a dishonest gain, and place such men over the people as rulers of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens, and let them judge the people at all times (Exodus 18:17–21). The large matter was decomposed into smaller matters, each entrusted to a smaller group, each accountable. The structure was not a hierarchy of aggregation but a network of delegated judgment.

The body of Christ is likewise one body with many members, each with its own function: if the foot should say, because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body, that would not make it any less a part of the body. If the ear should say, because I am not an eye, I do not belong to the body, that would not make it any less a part of the body (1 Corinthians 12:15–16). The whole depends on the parts, and each part on the whole, but no part aggregates the others — each serves its own function and is bound by the others.

The referendum paradox cannot arise, because no question is ever decided by more than one triad — a dissolution rather than a fix. Revisiting becomes a graph query: each decision records what it rested on, and when a parent is re-decided, descendants are flagged. Circular dependencies become detectable, and can be broken by identifying which decision must be made on a stated assumption.

Where triad C's decision depends on rulings by triads A and B, C is bound by them, which is premise-based aggregation distributed across groups. C may therefore be pushed toward a conclusion its own members would not independently reach. The proposed resolution is that a bound triad may formally reopen a prior decision rather than override it: prior rulings cannot be ignored, but can be sent back to their owners. This is designed, not built, and not yet tested.

Any decision spawning children should retain a closing decision reviewing the whole. This is specified and not yet implemented.

7 Tracking outcomes

At the next meeting, check where you are with last week's outcomes. Any triad member can mark an outcome as complete.

If there is disagreement, that can be recorded as in the normal procedure. Dissent does not require a reason, although it is strongly recommended.

This is the competence signal. Over time, the organisation can see who has been right, on what, and how often. That tells them who to route decisions to next time.

Scripture testifies that the accumulation of wisdom is a reaching toward truth. The heart of the wise seeks knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks wisdom (Proverbs 18:15). Wisdom is not given once; it is accumulated, tested, and refined through what comes to pass. The beginning of wisdom is this: get wisdom. Whatever you get, get insight (Proverbs 4:7). The accumulation — recording what was decided, what it rested on, and how it turned out — is the practice of that accumulation.

The way of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day (Proverbs 4:18). The light does not come from never stumbling. It comes from each step being recorded, examined, and corrected.

8 The amendment window

The amendment window stays open after the meeting. Each member of the triad can change their mind on something they said during the meeting. They must say what they would change about their own position and why. Not just "I disagree" — a reasoned case.

The closure period is determined by the Group. In many cases it may be a matter of minutes after the meeting. After that, the window closes and the decision stands — unless an outcome proves it wrong.

Two modes

Correction mode: a triad member changes their position on a condition they voted on, and the decision outcome changes as a result.

Reconsideration mode: a triad member says they were wrong to dissent, and the decision should have passed. The second is the harder one, because it requires someone to say "I got it wrong by saying no." It needs protection — procedural weight, not just a button.

Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in your power to do it (Proverbs 3:27). False rejection is withholding good. The structure should make it as easy to correct an error of exclusion as an error of commission.

An unresolved condition that later proves to have been the wrong condition to block is a form of false rejection — the condition was treated as unanswerable when it was answerable, and the decision was blocked as a result. The record should tag this too, because it is a distinct failure: the triad lacked the knowledge to resolve the condition, and the decision died on that lack. Correcting it means routing the decision to people who can resolve it, or decomposing the condition until it is answerable.

Dissent is the Gideon-like "I told you so." The record preserves the original decision and reasoning, so the person who was overruled is not erased but positioned to be proved right. The structure does not guarantee the righteous will be heard first. It guarantees that when they are proved right, the record will show it.

Let us examine our ways and test them, and let us return to the Lord (Lamentations 3:40). Examining and returning are one act. The same mechanism that names the error is the mechanism that corrects it.

By their fruits you will know them (Matthew 7:15–20). The fruit does not just tell you the tree is bad. It tells you what kind of bad. When the outcome contradicts the expectation, the record should tag false acceptance versus false rejection. These are not the same failure, and correcting them requires different actions.

Which error is cheaper depends on reversibility. We treat it as a parameter rather than pretending there is a neutral default. But the deeper biblical answer is: neither error is the goal. The goal is to structure the decision so that whichever error you make, you can see it, name it, and correct it.

9 The role of the system

The AI transcribes discussion, identifies decisions and their conditions, and infers each participant's position. Every inference is presented for human confirmation and can be overwritten.

This is a substantial role, and it creates a genuine tension with the principle that machine systems should not hold decision authority. Whoever frames the question exercises real influence over the answer, and here the model does part of the framing.

The response is measurement rather than assertion. Explicit voting is available and optional. Where participants vote, those votes serve as ground truth for the inference, and the resulting accuracy figure is published to the organisation. An organisation can begin with explicit voting and move to inference when the accuracy justifies it.

Scripture instructs testing and verification. Test everything; hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Test the spirits to see whether they are from God (1 John 4:1). The system's inferences are offered for testing, not for worship. They are presented to the triad, who may confirm or overwrite. The accuracy figure is published so the organisation can judge for itself whether the inference has earned trust.

Wisdom is also described as a quality to be sought and tested: if you cry for discernment and lift your voice for understanding, if you seek it like silver and search for it as for hidden treasures, then you will understand the fear of the Lord and find the knowledge of God (Proverbs 2:3–5). The system is a tool in the search; the triad is the seeker.

Trust in thy friend, and let him not fail thee: and if thou knowest what he doeth, be thou like unto him (Sirach 6:5–6). The system, like any counsellor, earns trust by what it does, not by what it claims. Trust is placed in what is tested, not in what is asserted.

—

10 The accountability structure

The structure does not prevent error. It prevents error from being permanent, invisible, or unnameable. That is what accountability means — not the absence of failure, but the presence of a path back.

Each of us will give an account of himself to God (Romans 14:12). The record makes that account possible — not perfect, but present.
