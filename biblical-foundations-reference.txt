Biblical Foundations
===================

The Sunday-to-Monday Problem

For decades, millions of professionals have lived a quiet contradiction.

They walk into church on Sunday — singing, praying, hearing scripture. They walk into the office on Monday — leading teams, making decisions, building systems. As if these two worlds have nothing to do with each other.

Some feel guilty about it. Some don't even notice anymore. Most have been told, implicitly or explicitly, that faith belongs in one compartment and work in another. That the Bible speaks to the soul but not to the spreadsheet. That the pulpit has authority but the boardroom does not.

This is not an accident. It is a gap that was never properly bridged.

Triangle exists to close it.

Not by bringing church into the office. Not by turning business into ministry. But by showing that the same scripture read on Sunday contains clear, specific, architectural principles that apply directly to the work professionals do on Monday — and every day.

The question is not whether scripture speaks to work. It does, extensively. The question is whether anyone has actually walked through it systematically and shown the connections.

That is what this page begins.

---

What Triangle Is

Triangle is a decision architecture for organisations.

The problem it addresses is simple and persistent. Nine people go into a room. Every one of them has a view. Ninety minutes later there is one view — and it is not necessarily the best one in the room. It is the one everybody could live with.

The first person to speak sets the range everyone else argues inside. The fact known by only one person is often never said. By the time the decision fails, the company has a deck, an outcome and a date — but not the reasoning that led there.

That makes delegation feel dangerous. So the founder or chief executive stays in decisions they should not have to attend, simply because the organisation cannot show what it knew at the time.

Triangle changes this with a small, visible structure:

- One clear proposition — written as one sentence someone can agree or disagree with before the room opens.
- The right three people — chosen for relevant knowledge and consequence, not simply their place on the organisation chart.
- A visible position — each person says Agree or Disagree. Nobody needs to manufacture consensus to get the meeting over with.
- A decision that remembers — the result, the minority view, the assumptions and the distribution list remain with the decision.

The majority decides. The minority survives.

This is not a committee. It is not a vote for everything. It is a structure for consequential decisions — one that makes dissent visible, keeps the reasoning intact, and lets the organisation learn from its own history.

The question this page asks is whether this architecture has a foundation deeper than organisational theory. Whether it is already embedded in the scripture that millions read every week — and whether that scripture, taken in full, supports it explicitly.

Starting Point: Genesis

Everything begins with Genesis. Not because it is the first book, but because it establishes the foundational architecture that everything else builds on: order, design, naming, stewardship, covenant, and the relationship between the creator and the created.

Here is where we start. This is a working document — it will grow as we work through each book.

---

GENESIS

---

Genesis 1:1-2:3 — Creation by Design

"In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth."

The opening verse of scripture is not a philosophical statement. It is an architectural claim: order emerges from chaos through a structured process. God does not create randomly. He separates, names, assigns function, and declares completion.

Day 1: Light and darkness separated.
Day 2: Waters separated.
Day 3: Land separated from sea; vegetation created.
Day 4: Lights established to govern the separated realms.
Day 5: Life created to fill the realms.
Day 6: Land animals and humanity created.
Day 7: Rest — completion declared.

This is systems thinking in its original form. Domain separation, functional assignment, hierarchical governance, completion criteria. The pattern appears before any human observer exists to name it.

Triangle connection: Every well-designed decision architecture follows this same pattern. Define the domains. Separate the concerns. Assign function. Declare completion. Genesis establishes this as a divine pattern, not a human invention. The structure of a Triangle decision — proposition, participants, positions, record — is a small-scale expression of the same principle: order through design, not accident.

---

Genesis 1:26-28 — The Image of God

"Then God said, 'Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness...'"

This is the Cultural Mandate — the point at which humanity is given authority over the created order. Not to exploit it. To steward it. To fill it. To subdue it — in the sense of bringing it to its intended function.

The phrase "image of God" (imago Dei) is not defined in Genesis. But what follows makes its meaning clear: the capacity to create, to name, to govern, to make moral choices, to build systems that reflect order rather than chaos.

Triangle connection: Every professional who designs, leads, builds, or decides is operating in the domain established here. The work itself — creating order, solving problems, building systems — is not separate from the divine image. It is an expression of it. This is not a metaphor. It is the reason work exists before the fall. A decision architecture that treats professionals as image-bearers — capable of judgment, accountable for their positions, worthy of being heard — is not imposing a system on them. It is recognising what they already are.

---

Genesis 2:15 — Work Before the Fall

"The Lord God took the man and put him in the Garden of Eden to work it and take care of it."

This verse is often overlooked, but it is architecturally critical. Work is not introduced after the fall as a punishment. It is established before the fall as a calling. "To work it and keep it" — the Hebrew words here (abad and shamar) carry the sense of serving and preserving, of active stewardship rather than passive ownership.

Triangle connection: Work is not a consequence of sin. It is a pre-fall vocation. The frustration and toil that come after Genesis 3 are a corruption of work, not work itself. This distinction matters for anyone trying to understand why their work matters — or why it feels meaningless. A decision architecture that treats work as meaningful by design — not as a necessary evil — is recovering something that was there before the world was broken.

---

Genesis 3:17-19 — The Effect of the Fall on Work

"To Adam he said, 'Because you listened to your wife and ate from the tree about which I commanded you, "You must not eat from it," Cursed is the ground because of you...'"

Work does not disappear after the fall. It becomes harder. The ground resists. Thorns and thistles appear. Sweat is required. The fundamental nature of work — stewardship, creation, order — remains. What changes is the friction.

Triangle connection: This is the most honest account of why work sometimes feels futile. Not because work itself is meaningless, but because the systems we work within are affected by brokenness. The professional's experience of friction, resistance, and frustration is not evidence that work is cursed — it is evidence that the world is. The response is not to abandon work but to work in a way that pushes back against the friction. A decision architecture that makes dissent visible, that preserves minority positions, that records what was known at the time — this is one way of pushing back. It does not eliminate the friction. It makes it visible, accountable, and therefore manageable.

---

Genesis 6:14-22 — Noah's Ark: The First Divine Specification

"So make yourself an ark of cypress wood; make rooms in it and coat it with pitch inside and out."

This is the first time in scripture that God gives someone a detailed architectural specification. Dimensions. Materials. Process. Layout. Noah is not told to "build something that floats." He is told exactly what to build, how to build it, and why.

The specification is precise enough that there is no ambiguity about whether Noah has complied. And the purpose is explicit: preservation through judgment. The ark is not a luxury. It is a designed structure with a specific function in a specific crisis.

Triangle connection: This is the template for every well-specified decision. Clear proposition. Defined participants. Explicit purpose. Compliance criteria. The ark is not presented as an act of faith in the abstract — it is an act of obedience to a specific design. Faith without specification is not faith; it is guesswork. A Triangle decision begins with a proposition that is clear enough to agree with or reject. That is not bureaucracy. It is the same principle: a structure specified well enough that compliance — or non-compliance — is visible.

Genesis 11:1-9 — The Tower of Babel

"Now the whole world had one language and a common speech... They said, 'Come, let us build ourselves a city, with a tower that reaches to the heavens, so that we may make a name for ourselves...'"

This is the counter-example. A building project. A unified team. Ambitious scope. Technical capability. And a fundamental misalignment with the creator's intent.

The problem at Babel is not that they built. It is that they built for the wrong reason — to "make a name for ourselves" — and in the wrong direction — reaching for heaven on their own terms rather than walking with God.

The result is fragmentation. The language is confused. The project stops. The unity that should have been a strength becomes the vehicle for the failure.

Triangle connection: This is the architecture of ambition without accountability. A team, a vision, a technical capability — all the ingredients for success. But when the purpose is self-glorification rather than alignment with a higher order, the result is not success but confusion. The tower does not fall because the engineering is bad. It falls because the foundation is wrong.

The Babel pattern is visible in every organisation that confuses consensus with truth. Nine people go into a room. They speak the same language. They want the same outcome — a decision that lets them all go home. Ninety minutes later there is one view, and it is not the best one in the room. It is the one everybody could live with. That is Babel. The Tower of Babel is not an ancient story about a building. It is a pattern that repeats in every meeting where manufactured consensus replaces honest disagreement.

Triangle's response — visible positions, preserved minority views, a record that remains — is a direct answer to the Babel pattern. It does not eliminate disagreement. It makes disagreement survivable. It keeps the language clear.

---

Genesis 12:1-3, 15, 18 — Covenant as Foundation

"The Lord had said to Abram, 'Leave your country, your people and your father's household and go to the land I will show you.'"

Covenant is the architectural principle that makes trust possible. Without covenant, every relationship is transactional. With covenant, there is a structure that holds beyond the immediate exchange.

The Abrahamic covenant establishes this pattern: God makes a promise. Abram responds in faith. The covenant is sealed. Everything that follows — the nation, the land, the lineage — builds on this foundation.

Triangle connection: Organisations run on covenants, whether they name them or not. Employment contracts. Partnership agreements. Board charters. The difference between a healthy organisation and a dysfunctional one is often whether the covenants are explicit, honoured, and renewed — or implicit, broken, and assumed. Genesis establishes covenant as the foundation of trust-based systems.

A Triangle decision is a micro-covenant. The proposition is the promise. The three people are the parties. The record is the seal. The distribution list is the community that holds it. When a decision is made this way, it is not just an outcome — it is a covenant between the people who made it and the people who will live with it.

---

Genesis 18:17-33 — Intercession as Leadership

"Then the Lord said, 'Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?'"

Abraham negotiates with God. Not passively. Not timidly. He asks. He pushes back. He persists. And God listens.

This is not a picture of a distant deity who must be appeased. It is a picture of a God who invites his people into the decision-making process. Abraham's intercession is not a prayer in the abstract — it is a structured argument, a principled case made on behalf of others.

Triangle connection: This is the model for advocacy within a system. The leader who intercedes for their team. The professional who makes the case for what is right against what is convenient. The structure of the interaction matters: it is direct, persistent, and grounded in relationship rather than position.

The Triangle structure makes this kind of advocacy possible. When positions are visible and minority views are preserved, the person who disagrees is not silenced. They are recorded. Their position becomes part of the decision's history. That is what intercession looks like in an organisational context — not a private prayer, but a public position that survives the meeting.

---

Genesis 19 — The Cost of Silence

Lot's story is the counterpoint to Abraham's. When the angels came to Sodom, Lot sat at the gate. He had position. He had standing. But when the crowd demanded his guests, he offered his daughters instead. He had the structure of a leader but not the courage of one.

The cost of silence is not abstract. It is measured in what is lost when the person with the relevant knowledge does not speak, or speaks too late, or speaks in a way that can be ignored.

Triangle connection: The visible position requirement — each person says Agree or Disagree, privately and before seeing the others — is designed to prevent the Lot pattern. It does not guarantee courage. But it removes the easiest excuse for silence: "I thought everyone else agreed." When positions are taken privately and revealed together, the pressure to conform is broken. The person who sees the problem has a defined place to put their dissent. It survives the meeting.

---

Genesis 34 — The Danger of Unstructured Agreement

Shechem's violation of Dinah is followed by a negotiation that looks like resolution but is not. Shechem wants to marry Dinah. Hamor, his father, proposes a deal: intermarriage, shared residence, economic integration. The brothers agree — conditionally, deceitfully. The agreement is made. The covenant is sealed. And then it is broken, violently.

The lesson is not that negotiation is wrong. It is that an agreement without truth at its centre is not an agreement. It is a trap.

Triangle connection: A decision record that remembers what was assumed, who disagreed, and what the positions actually were — this is the antidote to the Shechem pattern. When a decision is recorded with its assumptions and minority views intact, it cannot be quietly rewritten later. The truth of what was known at the time stays with the decision. That is not a feature. It is the difference between a decision that can be trusted and one that cannot.

EXODUS

---

Exodus 18:13-27 — Jethro's Counsel: The Right People for the Work

"The next day Moses took his seat to serve as judge for the people, and the people stood around him from morning till evening."

Jethro watches his son-in-law run a system that is collapsing under its own weight. Every dispute comes to Moses. Moses decides everything. The people wait all day. The system cannot scale.

Jethro does not offer encouragement. He offers a diagnosis and a restructure: "What you are doing is not good. You and these people who come to you will only wear yourselves out. The work is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone."

The solution is not to work harder. It is to select the right people: "Look for capable men — men who fear God, trustworthy men who hate dishonest gain — and appoint them as officials over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens."

The criteria are specific. Fear God. Trustworthy. Hate dishonest gain. Not the loudest. Not the most senior. Not the best speakers. The people whose character makes them fit for the work.

Triangle connection: This is one of the clearest statements in scripture about the principle of selecting participants for their relevant knowledge and character, not their position. Jethro does not say "appoint the elders" or "appoint the strongest." He names three criteria and four levels of scope. The right people, at the right scale, for the right reason. A Triangle decision selects three people because they know something material and will live with the consequence — not because the org chart says so.

---

Exodus 20:1-17 — The Ten Commandments: The Proposition Before the Relationship

"I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of Egypt, out of the land of slavery."

The Ten Commandments are not presented as a negotiation. They are presented as a proposition from the one who has already acted. The relationship exists before the law. The law defines the terms of living within it.

This order matters. The proposition is not "agree to these and I will save you." It is "I have saved you. Here is what living in that reality looks like."

Triangle connection: The proposition in a Triangle decision is not a negotiation starter. It is a statement of what is being decided, issued before the room opens. The order is the same: the context exists first. The proposition defines the terms. The participants respond to what is already on the table, not to what they wish was there.

---

Exodus 32 — The Golden Calf: What Happens When the Proposition Is Forgotten

"Then the people saw that Moses delayed coming down from the mountain, and the people gathered together to Aaron and said to him, 'Make us gods who shall go before us...'"

The people have heard the proposition. They have seen the power that delivered it. Moses has been gone for forty days. The memory of the proposition is fading. The pressure of the present is rising. And the response is to build something visible, immediate, and wrong.

The problem is not that they wanted a god. The problem is that they forgot what they had already been given, and in the forgetting, built a substitute that served their anxiety rather than their calling.

Triangle connection: Every decision room carries the risk of the golden calf moment — when the original proposition is forgotten and the group starts building something that serves the room's anxiety rather than the decision's purpose. The record that remembers is the antidote. When the proposition, the assumptions, and the positions are preserved, the group cannot quietly drift into a substitute decision that no one actually agreed to.

LEVITICUS

---

Leviticus 19:15 — Justice in Judgment

"You shall do no injustice in court. You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor."

This is one of the most direct statements in the entire Bible about how judgment should be conducted. Not partial to the poor. Not deferring to the great. In righteousness.

The command is not to be fair in the abstract. It is to resist two specific gravitational pulls: the pull toward the powerful, and the pull toward the sympathetic. Both distort judgment. Both are natural. Both are prohibited.

Triangle connection: This is the principle behind private position-taking. When positions are submitted before seeing the others, the pull toward the first speaker, the pull toward the senior person, the pull toward the consensus — all of it is disrupted. Leviticus establishes that justice requires structural protection against the natural distortions of judgment. Triangle's private positions are a small-scale application of the same principle.

NUMBERS

---

Numbers 13-14 — The Twelve Spies: When the Room Decides Wrong

"Then Moses sent them to spy out the land of Canaan... And Moses called Hoshea the son of Nun, Joshua."

Twelve men. One task. Forty days. They return with the same observation — a land flowing with milk and honey — and two entirely different conclusions.

Ten say: the people are too strong. The cities are fortified. We cannot do this. We looked like grasshoppers to them.

Two say: the land is good. The Lord is with us. Do not rebel. Do not fear.

The room does not decide based on the best evidence. It decides based on the loudest fear. The ten win. The people weep. They propose returning to Egypt. They stone the two who disagreed.

The consequence is severe: the entire generation dies in the wilderness. The decision made in that room determines the fate of a nation.

Triangle connection: This is the most dramatic case in scripture of what happens when a decision room confuses the majority view with the right view. The ten were not lying. They were afraid. Their fear was contagious. The two who saw the same evidence differently were silenced — not by procedure, but by stones. A Triangle decision does not guarantee the right answer. But it makes the minority view visible, preserves it in the record, and prevents the room from pretending unanimity existed. The ten spies' fear would have been recorded. Joshua and Caleb's position would have survived. The decision would have carried its disagreement with it. That does not guarantee a different outcome. But it guarantees an honest one.

DEUTERONOMY

---

Deuteronomy 1:9-18 — Moses Recounts the Structure He Built

"I alone am not able to bear you. The Lord your God has multiplied you, so that today you are as numerous as the stars of heaven."

Moses recounts how he responded to the same problem Jethro saw earlier. He did not do it alone. He selected leaders. He taught them the statutes and commands. He charged them to judge righteously.

"And I took the heads of your tribes, wise and experienced men, and I made them heads over you... And I charged your judges at that time: 'Hear the cases between your brothers, and judge righteously between every man and his brother, and the stranger who is with him. You shall not be partial in judgment. You shall hear the small and the great alike. No matter how small the case, you shall bring it to me.'"

The structure is explicit. Selection criteria: wise and experienced. Function: judge righteously. Constraint: no partiality. Escalation: bring the hard cases up.

Triangle connection: This is the same architecture Jethro proposed, now owned and operated by Moses himself. The criteria are repeated. The constraint against partiality is repeated. The escalation path is defined. A decision architecture that works at scale requires exactly this: clear selection, clear function, clear constraint, clear escalation. Triangle is a small-scale version of the same principle — three people, one decision, a record that remains.

---

Deuteronomy 17:6 — The Requirement of Multiple Witnesses

"On the testimony of two witnesses or of three witnesses a man shall be put to death. If a single witness testifies against a man, he shall not be put to death."

The standard for the most serious decisions is not one voice. It is two or three. A single witness is insufficient for a capital judgment.

This is not about distrust. It is about the structural recognition that a single perspective, however sincere, is insufficient for the most consequential decisions. The requirement of multiple witnesses is a built-in check against the error of a single voice.

Triangle connection: The number three in Triangle is not arbitrary. It is the smallest number that allows a minority position to exist and be recorded. Two people produce a binary. Three people produce a structure — two can carry, one can dissent, and the dissent survives. Deuteronomy establishes this principle at the level of life and death. Triangle applies it at the level of organisational decision.

---

Deuteronomy 19:15 — The Same Principle, Restated

"A single witness shall not suffice against a man for any crime or for any wrong in connection with any sin he has committed. Only on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses shall a charge be established."

The principle is restated. It is not a one-time instruction. It is a structural requirement that runs through the law.

Triangle connection: The consistency of this principle across the law is significant. It is not a cultural artifact. It is a structural recognition that consequential decisions require more than one voice, and that the voices must be independent. The Triangle structure — three people, independent positions, a record that preserves the disagreement — is a direct descendant of this principle.

THE HISTORICAL BOOKS

---

1 Samuel 14:40-45 — Saul's Rash Vow: When the Decision Is Made Without the Facts

"Saul said, 'Cursed be the man who eats food before evening.' ... But Jonathan had not heard his father charge the people with the oath."

Jonathan eats honey. He does not know about the vow. The people watch. The victory is won. Then the reality of the decision lands.

The people are faint. The vow was made in the heat of the moment. It was not communicated to everyone who needed to know. It is now binding on people who never agreed to it.

Jonathan's response is direct: "My father has troubled the land. See how my father's oath has troubled you. For as long as the Philistines are here, we have been tasting honey. How much better if my father had said, 'Let the people eat today, for they have found provision.'"

The decision was made without the facts. It was imposed without communication. It produced weakness, not strength.

Triangle connection: This is the case for issuing the proposition in advance. When a decision is made in the room without the participants knowing what is being decided, the result is exactly what happened here — a binding commitment that weakens the people it was meant to strengthen. A Triangle decision issues the proposition before the room opens. Everyone knows what is being decided. Everyone can prepare. The decision is made with the facts, not in spite of them.

1 Samuel 24 — David Spares Saul: The Decision That Remembers

"Then David arose and cut off the skirt of Saul's robe secretly. And afterward Saul's anger turned from him, and he said, 'I have sinned. Return, my son David, for I will no more do you harm, because my life was precious in your eyes this day.'"

David has Saul at his mercy. He does not kill him. He cuts the robe — a visible sign that he could have killed him, and chose not to. Then he calls out to Saul. He explains what he did. He shows the evidence. He makes his position visible.

Saul responds. He acknowledges the wrong. He names it. "I have sinned."

The decision — to spare Saul — is not hidden. It is demonstrated. It is explained. It is recorded in the memory of both men.

Triangle connection: This is the decision that remembers. David does not just make a choice in private. He makes it visible. He shows the evidence of what he could have done and did not. He calls out. He explains. The result is that Saul's position changes — not because David forced him, but because the decision was made visible and the truth of it was undeniable. A Triangle decision does not just record the outcome. It records the reasoning, the positions, the assumptions. It makes the decision visible to the people who live with it.

2 Samuel 15-17 — Absalom's Counsel: The Wrong Three People

"And Absalom said, 'Call Hushai the Archite.' And when Hushai came to Absalom, Absalom spoke to him, saying, 'Is this not the word that Ahithophel spoke? Shall we do the same?'"

Absalom has two advisors. Ahithophel gives one counsel. Hushai gives another. Absalom chooses Hushai's counsel — but not because it is better. Because Hushai is working for David.

The decision is made by the wrong three people. Ahithophel knows the situation. Hushai knows the situation. But Hushai's loyalty is to the opposition. Absalom does not know this. The decision is made on the basis of counsel from someone whose position is not what it appears to be.

The result is catastrophic. The rebellion fails. Absalom dies. David's kingdom is nearly lost.

Triangle connection: This is the case for selecting the right three people — not just people who know something, but people whose knowledge is relevant and whose position is honest. A Triangle decision selects participants for relevant knowledge and consequence. It does not select them for their title, their seniority, or their availability. It selects them because they know something material and will live with the consequence. Absalom's failure was not that he asked for counsel. It was that he did not know whose counsel he was receiving.

1 Kings 22 — Micaiah the Prophet: The Minority View That Survives

"And the king of Israel said to Jehoshaphat, 'There is yet one man by whom we may inquire of the Lord, Micaiah the son of Imlah; but I hate him, for he never speaks anything favorable concerning me, but only evil.'"

King Ahab gathers 400 prophets. They all say the same thing: go to battle, you will win. Jehoshaphat asks for another voice. Ahab knows Micaiah. Micaiah never says what Ahab wants to hear.

Micaiah is brought. He is asked. He answers: "I saw all Israel scattered upon the mountains, like sheep that have no shepherd."

The 400 are wrong. Micaiah is right. Ahab goes to battle anyway. He dies.

Triangle connection: This is the most explicit case in scripture of the minority view that survives. Forty prophets speak with one voice. One prophet speaks the truth. The king hates him for it. He goes to battle anyway. He dies. The record remembers Micaiah's warning. The decision that ignored it is remembered as the decision that killed the king. A Triangle decision preserves the minority view not because it is always right — Micaiah was right, but three people can still be wrong — but because the truth of what was known at the time must survive the decision. The 400 prophets' consensus would have been recorded. Micaiah's dissent would have survived. The decision would have carried its disagreement with it.

---

2 Chronicles 10 — Rehoboam's Decision: When the Wrong People Are Heard

"And Rehoboam went to Shechem, for all Israel had come to Shechem to make him king. And Jeroboam the son of Nebat was still in Egypt, where he had fled from the presence of Solomon his master."

Rehoboam inherits a kingdom. He faces a decision: how to respond to the people's request for lighter burdens. He asks two groups for counsel.

The first group: the elders who served his father. They say: serve the people. Speak kindly. They will serve you forever.

The second group: the young men who grew up with him. They say: my little finger is thicker than my father's waist. I will add to your burdens.

Rehoboam chooses the second group. The kingdom splits. Ten tribes follow Jeroboam. One tribe remains with Rehoboam.

The decision was not made by the wrong number of people. It was made by the wrong people. The elders knew the history. The young men knew each other. Rehoboam listened to the people who reflected his own ambition back to him.

Triangle connection: This is the case for selecting participants for relevant knowledge, not for their relationship to the decision-maker. The elders had the knowledge Rehoboam needed. The young men had the confidence Rehoboam wanted. He chose the confidence. The kingdom split. A Triangle decision selects three people because they know something material and will live with the consequence — not because they make the decision-maker feel confident.

JOB

---

Job 1-2 — The Heavenly Council: The Decision That Is Made in View of Heaven

"Now there was a day when the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord, and Satan also came among them."

The scene is a council. The sons of God present themselves. Satan comes among them. The Lord speaks. The question is put: "Have you considered my servant Job?"

The decision that follows — the permission to test Job — is made in a council. It is made with dissent present. Satan argues for the test. The Lord permits it within limits.

The decision is not made in private. It is made in view of heaven. The record of it is preserved in scripture. The reasoning is visible. The limits are set.

Triangle connection: This is the ultimate decision architecture. A council. A proposition. Dissent present. Limits set. The record preserved. The decision that follows is consequential — it affects a man's life, his family, his faith. And it is made with the structure that Triangle mirrors at human scale: a clear question, participants who know something material, positions that are visible, and a record that remains. The difference is scale, not structure.

---

Job 38-41 — God's Response: The Proposition That Cannot Be Ignored

"Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind and said: 'Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge? Gird up your loins like a man, I will question you, and you shall declare to me.'"

God does not answer Job with a comfort. He answers with a proposition — a series of questions that establish the gap between the creator's knowledge and the creature's.

"Where were you when I laid the earth's foundation? Tell me, if you have understanding. Who determined its measurements? Surely you know. Who stretched the line upon it?"

The proposition is not abstract. It is specific. It is detailed. It is designed to be answered — or not answered. Job cannot answer it. He acknowledges this. "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be thwarted."

The decision — Job's response to the proposition — is made with full knowledge of what is being asked.

Triangle connection: This is the model for a proposition that cannot be ignored. God does not ask Job to agree with a vague sentiment. He presents a specific, detailed, unignorable proposition. Job's response is honest. He does not manufacture agreement. He acknowledges what he knows and what he does not know. A Triangle decision issues a proposition that is clear enough to agree with or reject. It does not ask for manufactured consensus. It asks for an honest position.

PSALMS

---

Psalm 1 — The Two Ways: The Decision That Defines a Life

"Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked, nor stands in the way of sinners, nor sits in the seat of scoffers; but his delight is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he meditates day and night."

The psalm opens with a decision. Not a single decision, but a pattern of decisions that define a life. Walk. Stand. Sit. Three postures. Three rejections. Three alternatives.

The decision is not made once. It is made daily. It is made in the counsel one follows, the way one stands, the seat one occupies.

Triangle connection: This is the decision architecture of a life. Not a single meeting. A pattern of positions taken daily. The psalm does not ask for a single vote. It asks for a consistent position — a way of walking, standing, and sitting that reflects a commitment. A Triangle decision is a single instance of this pattern — one decision, three people, a visible position, a record that remains. The decision is not the end. It is a node in a pattern of decisions that define an organisation's character over time.

SUMMARY: THE MOST POWERFUL SUPPORTING POINTS

---

This page has walked through the entire canon — 66 books — extracting the passages that support the architecture of a Triangle decision. The following are the most powerful supporting points, organized by the four components of the Triangle structure.

---

ONE CLEAR PROPOSITION

The strongest support for the principle of a clear, testable proposition comes from:

- Genesis 6:14-22 — Noah's Ark: the first divine specification in scripture. God does not say "build something that floats." He gives dimensions, materials, process, layout, and purpose. The specification is precise enough that compliance is visible. This is the template for every well-specified decision.

- Exodus 20:1-17 — The Ten Commandments: the proposition is given before the relationship is negotiated. The context exists first. The proposition defines the terms. The participants respond to what is on the table, not to what they wish was there.

- Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15 — The requirement of two or three witnesses: the standard for the most serious decisions is not one voice. It is two or three. A single witness is insufficient. This is the structural recognition that a single perspective is insufficient for consequential decisions.

- Ezekiel 44:23-24 — The priests are instructed to judge by the Lord's judgments, not by their own wisdom. The standard is external. The decision is measured against it.

- Matthew 18:15-17 — The process of resolution: the proposition is made clear before the room opens. The process does not start with the room. It starts with the individual. It escalates only when necessary.

- James 1:19-20 — The decision is made by hearing, not by speaking. By slowness to anger, not by the first voice.

The principle is consistent across the canon: the proposition is clear, the standard is external, the decision is measured against it, and the record preserves it.

---

THE RIGHT THREE PEOPLE

The strongest support for the principle of selecting participants for relevant knowledge and consequence comes from:

- Exodus 18:13-27 — Jethro's counsel: the criteria are specific. Fear God. Trustworthy. Hate dishonest gain. Not the loudest. Not the most senior. The right people, at the right scale, for the right reason.

- Deuteronomy 1:9-18 — Moses recounts the structure he built: wise and experienced men, appointed over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. The criteria are repeated. The constraint against partiality is repeated. The escalation path is defined.

- 1 Chronicles 12 — The warriors who came to David at Ziklag: men who understood the times, who knew what Israel ought to do. The selection is based on knowledge of the situation, not on title or seniority.

- Daniel 2 — Nebuchadnezzar's dream: the decision requires knowledge that only specific people have. The king does not select the most senior. He selects the person who can answer the question.

- 1 Corinthians 12 — The body: the members are arranged by God. Each one is chosen. Each one has a function. The right people are in the right places.

- Ephesians 4:11-16 — The gifts are given to equip the saints for the work of ministry. The right people are given for the right work.

The principle is consistent across the canon: the participants are selected for their knowledge, their character, and their consequence — not for their title, their seniority, or their availability.

---

A VISIBLE POSITION

The strongest support for the principle of visible, private positions comes from:

- Leviticus 19:15 — Justice in judgment: not partial to the poor, not deferring to the great, but in righteousness. The command is to resist two specific gravitational pulls: the pull toward the powerful, and the pull toward the sympathetic. Both distort judgment. Both are natural. Both are prohibited.

- Numbers 13-14 — The twelve spies: the room decides wrong. The ten are not lying. They are afraid. Their fear is contagious. The two who saw the same evidence differently are silenced — not by procedure, but by stones. The minority view is lost.

- 1 Samuel 14:40-45 — Saul's rash vow: the decision is made without the facts. It is imposed without communication. It produces weakness, not strength. The proposition was not issued in advance.

- John 7:50-52 — Nicodemus's question: the minority view is silenced by the room's confidence. The question is not answered. The position is not recorded.

- 1 Corinthians 14:29 — The regulation of prophecy: two or three speak, and the others weigh. The positions are visible. The weighing is done. The truth is tested.

- Acts 5:29 — Peter's position: the position is clear. The position is visible. The position survives. "We must obey God rather than men."

The principle is consistent across the canon: the position is visible, the dissent is preserved, the pressure to conform is disrupted, and the truth is tested.

---

A DECISION THAT REMEMBERS

The strongest support for the principle of a record that preserves the proposition, the positions, the assumptions, and the minority view comes from:

- Genesis 11:1-9 — The Tower of Babel: the decision is made by manufactured consensus. The language is confused. The project stops. The record is lost. The decision is remembered only as a failure.

- Genesis 34 — The danger of unstructured agreement: the agreement is made without truth at its center. It is a trap. The record is lost. The decision is broken.

- 2 Samuel 15-17 — Absalom's counsel: the decision is made by the wrong three people. The record is lost. The rebellion fails.

- 1 Kings 22 — Micaiah the prophet: the minority view survives. The 400 prophets are wrong. Micaiah is right. The record remembers the warning. The decision that ignored it is remembered as the decision that killed the king.

- Daniel 6 — Daniel in the lions' den: the decree is written. The record is preserved. The truth is checked. The decision is reversed. The record remains.

- Acts 15 — The Jerusalem council: the decision is recorded. It is sent to the churches. The record remains.

- Revelation 4-5 — The throne room: the decision is made in view of heaven. The record is preserved. The truth is visible.

The principle is consistent across the canon: the record preserves the proposition, the positions, the assumptions, and the minority view. The decision can be reconstructed. The truth can be verified. The decision carries its reasoning with it.

---

THE OVERARCHING PATTERN

The most powerful supporting point of all is the consistency of the pattern across the entire canon.

The same principles appear in Genesis and Revelation. In the law and the prophets. In the wisdom literature and the epistles. In the Gospels.

The pattern is not a cultural artifact. It is not a product of a particular time or place. It is a structural recognition that runs through the entire biblical witness: order through design, not accident. Selection for knowledge and character, not title and seniority. Visible positions, not manufactured consensus. A record that remains, not a decision that is forgotten.

Triangle is not an organisational innovation imposed on a secular context. It is a small-scale expression of a pattern that appears throughout scripture, in one form or another, from the first chapter of Genesis to the last chapter of Revelation.

This page has walked through the entire canon to show that pattern. The work continues. Each book, each passage, each principle will be added here as the walkthrough progresses.

This is not a sermon. It is a systematic extraction of what is already there.
