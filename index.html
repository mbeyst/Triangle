A decision architecture for organisations

Every organisation records what it decided. Almost none record why, who disagreed, or what it rested on.

The problem

Three things go wrong in the way organisations decide, and they compound.

The decision sits above the knowledge.

The person who understands the problem cannot rule on it. The person who can rule on it has less context. So the question climbs, waits for a calendar slot, gets decided by someone further from the facts, and travels back down degraded.

The reasoning is never kept.

Wikis and minutes record conclusions. They do not record the argument — who objected, on what grounds, and under what conditions they would have been right. So when a decision fails there is nothing to learn from except the outcome and whoever was standing nearest. That is how blame culture forms: not because people are unfair, but because the reasoning is missing and only the person is visible.

Nothing is connected.

No one can say what depends on what. The odd exception in the billing rules exists for a reason nobody remembers, so eighteen months later someone tidies it up and takes down invoicing.

Triangle addresses all three by fixing the record.

How it works

You do not change your meetings

Start here, because it is the thing most people assume wrongly.

Triangle does not replace your meetings, shrink them, or add a voting ceremony. Twelve people can be in the room. Nobody stops the discussion to fill in a form.

What changes is that each decision on the agenda has three people named against it. That is the whole structural ask.

01

Three people, chosen for the question

Decisions belong to a triad: three people named because they know something about this decision, not because of where they sit on a chart. Membership is per decision. Triads form and dissolve.

Three is deliberate. Adding people to a decision buys less accuracy than most executives assume, and costs accountability, speed and attention. Three is small enough that nobody can hide and large enough that no one decides alone.

Two of three carries. Dissent is recorded, not erased.

02

The discussion runs as it already does

People talk, argue, and reach a view. The system tracks four positions on each item: agree, disagree, neutral, or propose an alternative.

An alternative is not a vote. It reopens the question in a clearer form, which is often the most useful thing anyone does in a meeting.

03

The summary is the product

At the end, the system produces the record: what was decided, what it depends on, who owns what, and where each person stood.

Everyone reviews it and confirms or corrects their own position. The record stays open for twenty-four hours, so someone who thinks again overnight can amend it publicly, with a reason — the same way you would raise an objection after properly reading a contract.

Participants can also vote explicitly on screen. Where they do, those votes tell us how accurately the system reads a room, and that accuracy figure is published to you. Start with explicit votes. Move to inference when the number earns it. You never have to take it on trust.

04

Every decision records what it rests on

Each decision links to the decisions and assumptions it depends on. That single field is what makes the rest work.

When something a decision rested on changes, every decision downstream of it is flagged for review. Automatically. Nobody has to remember that the database choice depended on an assumption made two years ago by someone who has since left.

It also exposes circular dependencies — the budget that cannot be set until headcount is known, and the headcount that cannot be set until the budget is. Everyone has been in that loop. Triangle can name it, and tell you which decision has to be made on a stated assumption to break it.

05

Scaling without committees

Triangle never puts more people on a decision. It splits the decision instead.

A triad ruling on something large spawns the sub-decisions it does not own, and each goes to its own triad, in parallel. A triad that cannot decide because it is missing an input spawns that question and routes it sideways to whoever owns it — rather than escalating upwards to someone with less context.

The organisation ends up with a map of decisions and their dependencies. Every company has a chart of who reports to whom. None has a map of what depends on what.

What you get

A record you can defend

Named people, a stated question, positions, dissent preserved, and the reasoning intact. If AI contributed, it is logged as advisory and the humans who decided are named.

Shorter paths to decisions

Time from question to decision is measurable, before and after. It is the clearest number a pilot produces.

Delivery, tracked separately

Judgment and execution are different competences and Triangle keeps two records. Judgment informs who decides. Delivery informs who owns the work. They are never merged into a single score — a system that promotes the best operator into the decision seat is the problem, not the fix.

A ledger that compounds

Over time the record shows whose agreement means something — whether a person's yes reliably precedes a different outcome from their no. That is discrimination, and it is the part of judgment that cannot be trained or faked.

What Triangle does not do

It does not make decisions.

The system structures the record. People decide, and people can rewrite anything the system produced about them.

It does not score people on outcomes they did not control.

A good decision can have a bad outcome. The record separates them.

It cannot measure judgment in a pilot.

Separating a good decision-maker from a lucky one takes dozens of resolved decisions per person, not a handful. A pilot gives you the record and the latency numbers. The judgment layer is a second-year result, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something.

The judgment ledger can be gamed. The record is harder.

Be clear about which part of this is exposed.

Any system that scores people over time can be played — hedged positions, strategic neutrality, dissent registered for the file rather than for the argument. The judgment ledger is a scoring system. Defences are designed and not yet built, and until they are, that layer runs on participants engaging in good faith.

The decision record is a different thing. What the question was, who held which position, what it rested on, and when that stopped being true — that is a contemporaneous account confirmed by the people named in it, with a twenty-four hour correction window. Reconstructing a decision that has already happened cannot be gamed at all. It is history.

And for what it is worth: board minutes can be gamed. Performance reviews are gamed as a matter of routine. Risk registers are gamed by everyone who has ever written one. The difference is that we publish our attack surface. Ask your current system for its own.

It is not a replacement for judgment, culture or trust.

It is a communication tool. It makes agreement explicit, ownership unavoidable and reasoning durable. It does not manufacture any of the three.

The triad is a closed room

What is said inside a triad stays inside it. The decision and the outcome belong to the organisation. The deliberation belongs to the triad.

Management can request a room be opened. It is available, and it is micromanagement — the entire premise is that you trust three people you chose to work together without an audience.

To be clear about what this is: a policy control with an audit trail of who opened what, not encryption. Cryptographic enforcement is on the roadmap, not in a pilot.

Regulators, auditors and courts are not management.

The closed room is an internal norm, not a legal shield, and it never overrides a lawful requirement to disclose. Where a regulator, auditor, ombudsman or court can compel access to your records, they can compel access to these. Nothing in Triangle is designed to obstruct that, and nothing in it should be used to try. If your obligations require deliberation to be disclosable by default, we set that at the start rather than discovering it later.

Visibility settings are agreed per organisation before a pilot starts. A company that already records every meeting has different norms from one that does not, and Triangle adapts to that rather than dictating it.

Who it is for

Organisations where decisions are slow, ownership is vague, or the same arguments keep being relitigated.

It bites hardest where:

  • A board or regulator can ask you to demonstrate how a decision was reached, not just what was decided
  • The organisation has grown past the point where everyone knows everyone, and decisions that used to happen in corridors now queue for calendar slots
  • Ownership has recently changed — new investor, merger, new chief executive — and nobody can reconstruct why the inherited decisions were made
  • The same question keeps coming back because the last answer was never written down with its reasoning

It works best where people are prepared to be clear: to say agree, disagree, neutral, or here is a better version of the question. That is a trainable habit and it is part of what a pilot delivers. Every participant reads a short guide first. Nobody is asked to change how they work beyond that.

Pilots

The framework is designed and ready to run in a real organisation.

Twenty decisions.
Fixed scope. £5,000.

Twenty decisions rather than thirty days, because a calendar window measures how often your board meets, not how well your organisation decides. At executive team cadence, twenty decisions usually takes four to six weeks.

The pilot runs with your executive team, not your board. That is where the decisions are.

Phase one — one decision that already went wrong

Before anything changes, we take a decision you have already made that did not work out, and reconstruct the record that should have existed. What the question actually was. Who held which position. What it rested on. When that assumption stopped being true, and what downstream should have been flagged and was not.

It requires no new behaviour from anyone. It is usually the point at which people understand what the rest of this is for.

Phase two — your next twenty decisions

A working decision record

Questions, positions, dissent, dependencies and owners.

A measured change in decision latency

Time from question to decision, before and after, as a number.

A delivery record

What was committed to, by whom, and what was completed.

A written debrief

What worked in your context, what did not, and what we would change. Including the parts that did not work — that is the point of a pilot.

We run one pilot at a time. That is deliberate. A pilot involves facilitation, training every participant, and a full written debrief. Run several at once and none of them gets done properly.

One side effect worth mentioning, though it is not the reason to do this. Naming three people against each decision tends to reveal how many people are sitting in meetings about decisions they do not own. Most organisations find the calendar cost of that exceeds the fee. It is a pleasant side effect of a record that makes ownership explicit, not a productivity product.
