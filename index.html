A decision architecture for organisations

Every organisation records what it decided. Almost none record why, who disagreed, or what it rested on.

The problem

Three things go wrong in the way organisations decide, and they compound.

The decision sits above the knowledge. The person who understands the problem cannot rule on it. The person who can rule on it has less context. So the question climbs, waits for a calendar slot, gets decided by someone further from the facts, and travels back down degraded.

The reasoning is never kept. Wikis and minutes record conclusions. They do not record the argument — who objected, on what grounds, and under what conditions they would have been right. So when a decision fails there is nothing to learn from except the outcome and whoever was standing nearest. That is how blame culture forms: not because people are unfair, but because the reasoning is missing and only the person is visible.

Nothing is connected. No one can say what depends on what. The odd exception in the billing rules exists for a reason nobody remembers, so eighteen months later someone tidies it up and takes down invoicing.

Triangle addresses all three by fixing the record.

How it works

01Three people, chosen for the question

Decisions belong to a triad: three people selected because they know something about this decision, not because of where they sit on a chart. Membership is per decision. Triads form and dissolve.

Three is deliberate. Adding people to a decision buys less accuracy than most executives assume, and costs accountability, speed and attention. Three is small enough that nobody can hide and large enough that no one decides alone.

Two of three carries. Dissent is recorded, not erased.

02The meeting runs normally

There is no voting ceremony. Nobody stops the discussion to fill in a form. People talk, argue, and reach a view — as they already do.

The system listens and tracks four positions on each item: agree, disagree, neutral, or propose an alternative. An alternative is not a vote. It reopens the question in a clearer form, which is often the most useful thing anyone does in a meeting.

03The summary is the product

At the end, the system produces the record: what was decided, what it depends on, who owns what, and where each person stood.

Everyone reviews it and confirms or corrects their own position. The record stays open for twenty-four hours, so someone who thinks again overnight can amend it publicly, with a reason — the same way you would raise an objection after properly reading a contract.

Participants can also vote explicitly on screen. Where they do, those votes tell us how accurately the system reads a room, and that accuracy figure is published to you. Start with explicit votes. Move to inference when the number earns it. You never have to take it on trust.

04Every decision records what it rests on

Each decision links to the decisions and assumptions it depends on. That single field is what makes the rest work.

When something a decision rested on is changed, every decision downstream of it is flagged for review. Automatically. Nobody has to remember that the database choice depended on an assumption made two years ago by someone who has since left.

It also exposes circular dependencies — the budget that cannot be set until headcount is known, and the headcount that cannot be set until the budget is. Everyone has been in that loop. Triangle can name it, and tell you which decision has to be made on a stated assumption to break it.

05Scaling without committees

Triangle never puts more people on a decision. It splits the decision instead.

A triad ruling on something large spawns the sub-decisions it does not own, and each goes to its own triad, in parallel. A triad that cannot decide because it is missing an input spawns that question and routes it sideways to whoever owns it — rather than escalating upwards to someone with less context.

The organisation ends up with a map of decisions and their dependencies. Every company has a chart of who reports to whom. None has a map of what depends on what.

What you get

A record you can defend

Named people, a stated question, positions, dissent preserved, and the reasoning intact. If AI contributed, it is logged as advisory and the humans who decided are named.

Shorter paths to decisions

Time from question to decision is measurable, before and after. It is the clearest number a pilot produces.

Delivery, tracked separately

Judgment and execution are different competences and Triangle keeps two records. Judgment informs who decides. Delivery informs who owns the work. They are never merged into a single score — a system that promotes the best operator into the decision seat is the problem, not the fix.

A ledger that compounds

Over time the record shows whose agreement means something — whether a person's yes reliably precedes a different outcome from their no. That is discrimination, and it is the part of judgment that cannot be trained or faked.

The triad is a closed room

What is said inside a triad stays inside it. The decision and the outcome belong to the organisation. The deliberation belongs to the triad.

Management can request a room be opened. It is available, and it is micromanagement — the entire premise is that you trust three people you chose to work together without an audience.

To be clear about what this is: a policy control with an audit trail of who opened what, not encryption. Cryptographic enforcement is on the roadmap, not in a pilot.

Visibility settings are agreed per organisation before a pilot starts. A company that already records every meeting has different norms from one that does not, and Triangle adapts to that rather than dictating it.

What Triangle does not do

It does not make decisions. The system structures the record. People decide, and people can rewrite anything the system produced about them.

It does not score people on outcomes they did not control. A good decision can have a bad outcome. The record separates them.

It cannot measure judgment in a month. Separating a good decision-maker from a lucky one takes dozens of resolved decisions per person, not a handful. In a thirty-day pilot you get the record and the latency numbers. The judgment layer is a second-year result, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something.

It can be gamed in its current form. Any system that measures people can be played. We have designed defences and they are not built yet. A pilot runs on the understanding, agreed beforehand, that participants engage with it in good faith. The next version does not rely on that.

It is not a replacement for judgment, culture or trust. It is a communication tool. It makes agreement explicit, ownership unavoidable and reasoning durable. It does not manufacture any of the three.

Who it is for

Organisations where decisions are slow, ownership is vague, or the same arguments keep being relitigated because nobody recorded why they were settled.

It works best where people are prepared to be clear: to say agree, disagree, neutral, or here is a better version of the question. That is a trainable habit and it is part of what a pilot delivers. Every participant reads a short guide first. Nobody is asked to change how they work beyond that.

Pilots

The framework is designed and ready to run in a real organisation.

Thirty days. Fixed scope. £5,000.

A working decision record

Covering the decisions your organisation makes during the period — questions, positions, dissent, dependencies and owners.

A measured change in decision latency

Time from question to decision, before and after, as a number.

A delivery record

What was committed to, by whom, and what was completed.

A written debrief

What worked in your context, what did not, and what we would change. Including the parts that did not work — that is the point of a pilot.

We run one pilot at a time. That is deliberate. A pilot involves facilitation, training every participant, and a full written debrief. Run several at once and none of them gets done properly.
