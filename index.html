Triangle Protocol

A way to make consequential decisions in a small group, record how they were reached, and keep that record intact.

Process Record DAG Tiers Facilitators Group Cycle Breaking Biblical

The unit is a triad — three people, one decision. The decision is written as one sentence. Each person votes on each condition the decision rests on. Two of three carries it. The dissent is recorded. The record stays with the decision.

The Process

1 Surface the decision
Write it as one sentence. If it has "and" in it, split it into two decisions.
Bad: "Let's talk about the budget."
Good: "Do we approve the budget for the new project?"
If the sentence is not clear enough to agree or disagree with, it is not ready.
2 Choose a facilitator
The facilitator structures the agenda, frames the conditions, and closes with a review. They do not chair the meeting — they design it. The facilitator is also a voter.
The person who surfaced the decision usually facilitates. If someone else is better suited, that person facilitates instead. If the group cannot agree, the authorised person selects.
3 Form the triad
Ask the group: who are the best three people for this decision?
Anyone who wants to be in it volunteers. Anyone willing to take part is eligible.
Exactly three? You're set. More than three and divisible by three? Form that many triads. Not divisible by three? Draw lots for the remaining spots. No one volunteers? The authorised person picks three.
Summoning an outsider: The triad may summon an outsider to speak to a condition. The outsider gives input, not a vote. The summoning is recorded.
4 Assess the tier
The CEO decides the tier initially, with facilitator input.
Tier 1 (Operational): Single triad, 2-of-3. Day-to-day decisions.
Tier 2 (Tactical): Multiple triads, higher threshold. Cross-functional decisions.
Tier 3 (Strategic): Many triads, 66% of organization's triads. Company-wide decisions.
As the organization matures, the Facilitators Group takes on more of this responsibility.
5 Frame the conditions
Before anyone votes, the facilitator names the conditions the decision rests on.
"What has to be true for this to be the right call?"
Write each condition as a yes/no question. Keep it to two or three. If there are more, the decision needs splitting.
The triad can challenge the conditions before voting. The conditions are not imposed — they are stated by a person who is also a voter.
Summoning an outsider: The triad may summon an outsider to speak to a condition. The outsider gives input, not a vote.
6 Vote — two of three
Each person votes on each condition: yes, no, or neutral.
Two out of three yes votes on any resolved condition means it passes. Zero yes votes means it fails.
Neutral vote: "I do not have a position on this condition." Not maybe. Not abstention. An honest signal that the condition is not answerable by this person.
A condition needs at least two non-neutral votes to be resolved. If fewer, it is unresolved and the decision cannot pass on it.
If all three agree, record it as unanimous. That is the strongest signal. But it is not required.
The dissent is recorded. Who said no, and why.
7 Leave the window open
The amendment window stays open after the meeting.
Each member of the triad can change their mind on something they said during the meeting. They must say what they would change about their own position and why — a reasoned case.
The closure period is determined by the facilitator. It is public to everyone in the company. Default options: immediate or 24 hours.
After that, the window closes and the decision stands — unless an outcome proves it wrong.
8 Track it over time
At the next meeting, check where you are with last week's outcomes. Any triad member can mark an outcome as complete.
If there is disagreement, that can be recorded. Dissent does not require a reason, although it is strongly recommended.
This is the competence signal. Over time, you see who has been right, on what, and how often. That tells you who to route decisions to next time.

Faithfulness Data

The following data is collected for each person from the digital records:

  • Attendance: Did they attend the meeting?
  • Commitment: Did they vote (not neutral)? Did they track outcomes? Did they revisit their own decisions?
  • Facilitator volunteering: Did they volunteer to facilitate?

This data is collected automatically. It is visible to everyone in the company.

Purpose: This data encourages people to dissent only when they truly feel dissent, rather than trying to rack up points by being the person who disagrees. It also identifies who shows up and who doesn't.

This is not a scoring system. It is not a reward system. It is not a punishment system. It is data for people to observe.

Phase II design: How to usefully score and interpret this data is a separate design specification. It is beyond the basic protocol.

The Record

Every decision is written down. Not a summary. The actual record.

Decision: [one sentence] Tier: [operational / tactical / strategic] Conditions: [list] Vote per condition: [who voted what — yes, no, or neutral] Result: [approved / not approved / unresolved] Dissent: [who dissented, and why] Expected outcome: [what we expect to happen if we're right] Date: [today] Triad: [three names] After the outcome: Outcome: [what actually happened] Match: [yes / no / too early to tell] Date checked: [when]

The record is not overwritten when a decision is revisited. The original stands, marked as revisited, with the reasoning and the correction. That is accountability — not the absence of failure, but the presence of a path back.

If It Goes Wrong

False acceptance

You approved something that failed — flag it. Name what was wrong. Reopen the decision. Send it back to the triad that decided it. They cannot be overruled — but they can be asked to reconsider.

False rejection

You blocked something that would have worked — someone has to say "we got it wrong by saying no." Give it procedural weight. Let them make the case. Let the triad reopen.

Either way: the original decision stays in the record. It is not erased. It is marked as revisited, with the reasoning and the correction.

For the Facilitator

You do not chair the meeting. You design it.

Before the meeting

  • List the decisions, not the topics.
  • Decompose each decision into its conditions.
  • Identify who should be in the room for each.
  • Sequence by dependency — if B depends on A, A goes first.
  • Assess the tier with the CEO.

During the meeting

  • Frame each decision and its conditions.
  • Let the triad challenge the framing before voting.
  • Record the vote, the dissent, and the expected outcome.
  • Ask: is there a voice we have not heard?

After the meeting

  • Keep the amendment window open.
  • Track the outcome.
  • Feed what you learned into the next meeting.

The meeting is not the container. The decision is the container. The meeting is the shape the decision takes when the right people are brought together for it.

For Multiple Decisions — The DAG

When a meeting has several decisions, the agenda is not a list — it is a routing problem.

Each decision is a node. Edges record dependency: premise, constraint, or delegation. The structure is a directed acyclic graph.


A triad ruling on a large question spawns the sub-decisions it does not own, each routed to its own triad, in parallel.

A triad blocked for want of an input spawns the blocking question and routes it laterally to whoever owns it, rather than escalating to a less-informed authority.

Any decision spawning children should retain a closing decision reviewing the whole.

Cycle Breaking

If a cycle is detected, the facilitator chooses:

(a) Temporary assumption

The triad resolves with a documented assumption, proceeds, and the revisit alert catches violations when the assumption is later violated.

(b) Competent triad

The condition is routed to the triad with actual competence to answer it.

In either case, the triad may summon outsiders to inform their decision without expanding the meeting.

Closing Review

The facilitator decides the closing review period. It is public to everyone in the company. Default options: immediate or 24 hours.

The parent triad reconvenes after all children resolve. They vote again with all child outcomes as conditions. The decision closes.


If a child hasn't resolved within the closing review period, the parent triad can proceed with a temporary assumption. The unresolved child becomes a constraint on the parent, and the revisit alert fires when it finally resolves.


Member change doesn't matter. The decision is respected, not the people. If the parent triad members have changed, the new members inherit the decision.

The Facilitators Group

A standing pool of facilitators, available on demand. Not a standing committee. It exists to be drawn from, not to convene.

Formation: Follows the same protocol as triad formation — volunteer first, then draw lots, then authorised person picks. No special rules.

Two triads in conflict

  • Draw one representative from each interfacing triad
  • Draw one from the Facilitators Group (by lot)
  • The three form a conflict resolution triad
  • Two-of-three decides
  • Procedural only — impose an ordering, set a temporary assumption, route to competence

Three or more triads in conflict

  • Each triad's facilitator forms a new triad
  • Numbers not divisible by three resolved the same way as initial triad formation (draw lots)
  • Same procedural scope

Rejection Clause

If a triad rejects the Facilitators Group's decision, the decision stands. The dissent is recorded as a refusal to accept the process.


Refusal is data. It is visible to everyone in the company. It could indicate competence — the person sees something others don't. It could indicate something else. Over time, patterns emerge.

Existing senior management observes these patterns and implements relevant consequences where appropriate.

The protocol does not automate consequences. It makes the data available for human judgment.

Tiered Autonomy

The principle: The more triads that agree on a decision, the more autonomous that decision becomes. Harder to make, harder to unmake. The breadth of agreement determines the decision's independence.

Tier 1 — Operational

Single triad, 2-of-3

Day-to-day decisions: approve a budget, hire for a role, choose a vendor.

Closing review: immediate or 24 hours. Amendment window: short. Revisit threshold: same triad that made it.

Autonomy: low — easy to revisit, easy to correct.

Weight: One triangle. More autonomy than one person, but cannot overthrow predetermined structure.

Tier 2 — Tactical

Multiple triads, higher threshold

Cross-functional decisions: expand to a new market, launch a product, restructure a team.

Requires all interfacing triads to agree on each condition (default: 2-of-3 per triad).

Closing review: facilitator decides, default 48-72 hours. Amendment window: longer.

Revisit threshold: requires the same multi-triad consensus to reopen.

Autonomy: medium — harder to revisit, requires broader agreement to change.

Weight: Multiple triangles. More like a covenant. Challenging a policy or procedure requires this level.

Tier 3 — Strategic

Many triads, highest threshold

Company-wide decisions: acquire a company, enter a new industry, change the mission.

Requires 66% of the organization's triads to agree.

Facilitators Group sign-off confirms process was followed.

Closing review: facilitator decides, default 1 week. Amendment window: longest.

Revisit threshold: requires equivalent multi-triad consensus to reopen.

Autonomy: high — very hard to revisit, requires equivalent weight of agreement to overturn.

Weight: Many triangles. Functions like a covenant. Overturning requires the same breadth of agreement.

Who decides the tier: Initially, the CEO decides. As the organization matures with Triangle, they lean more on its principles — the Facilitators Group takes on more of this responsibility.

Records and the DAG

All decisions recorded digitally. The DAG is generated from the records automatically — nodes are decisions, edges are dependencies (premise, constraint, delegation), lateral routes are recorded as spawned nodes.

Paper-based use is possible but loses the DAG generation.

Why It Works

Three people are better than one when each is more likely right than wrong. Below that line, adding more people makes the decision worse. The triad captures most of the available gain before the assumptions underwriting it break down, at a fraction of the cost.

Two of three is the minimal structure that treats every voice the same and both sides of a question the same. A supermajority threshold privileges the status quo. Unanimity lets one person hold the whole group. Two of three prefers the outcome that moves, and records the dissent, to the outcome that does not move because one person objected.

Recording per condition corrects a measurable bias: because concluding "yes" on a conjunctive decision requires being right about every condition, conclusion-level voting rejects sound multi-condition proposals at a higher rate than the evidence warrants.

The amendment window catches a decision going wrong before the outcome arrives. Revisit alerts flag descendants when a parent is re-decided, so the error is seen not just at its source but everywhere it landed.

The structure does not prevent error. It prevents error from being permanent, invisible, or unnameable.

Biblical Comparison

The protocol rediscovered biblical patterns through a different reasoning path. The structure is sound because it aligns with how God designed human beings to make decisions together.

Parallels

Deuteronomy 19:15 — Two or three witnesses. The triad of three and the 2-of-3 threshold. Same shape, different justification.

Ecclesiastes 4:9-12 — Threefold cord not quickly broken. The triad as a cord of three strands.

Matthew 18:15-17 — Step-wise escalation. Private → witnesses → church. Maps to triad → lateral route → Facilitators Group.

Acts 15 — The Jerusalem Council. Cross-community dispute resolved by a convened council.

Exodus 18 — Jethro's advice to Moses. Appoint able men to judge. Maps to the Facilitators Group as a pool of competent people available on demand.

Proverbs 16:33 — Casting lots. The lot is cast into the lap. Maps to draw lots for triad formation and Facilitators Group selection.

Proverbs 11:14, 15:22, 24:6 — Abundance of counselors. Where there is no guidance, a people falls.

Proverbs 27:17 — Iron sharpens iron. The triad as mutual sharpening.

Jeremiah 32 — The deed of purchase. Recorded deed, sealed, stored in an earthenware jar.

Ruth 4 — The legal transaction. Witnesses, recorded deed, signed document.

Genesis 41 — Joseph's interpretation. Recorded dreams, tracked over seven years.

Psalm 15 — Who may dwell in God's presence. Speaks truth, does no wrong, casts no slur, takes no bribe.

Micah 6:8 — Do justice, love kindness, walk humbly.

Galatians 6:1 — Restore in a spirit of gentleness.

Proverbs 3:9 — Honor the LORD with firstfruits. Give the best portion first.

What the Bible adds

Covenant framing. The Bible frames agreements as covenants — binding, relational, with signs. The protocol frames decisions as records — factual, procedural.

Periodic reset (Jubilee). Every 50 years, debts are forgiven, land returns. The protocol has revisit alerts but no periodic reset.

Lament space. Psalms are laments — expressing grief and confusion. The protocol records dissent but doesn't have a space for lament.

Community consequences. 1 Corinthians 5 — expel the wicked. The protocol's rejection clause is softer — decision stands, dissent recorded, visible to all.

Shalom as the goal. The biblical concept of wholeness and peace. Good decisions, done well, in community, with accountability, produce shalom as a byproduct.

What the protocol adds

The neutral vote. "I don't have a position on this condition." Novel concept with no direct biblical parallel.

The DAG structure. Dependency mapping for multiple decisions.

The amendment window. A time-limited ability to change one's mind after a decision is made.

The competence tracking. Who has been right, on what, and how often.

The lateral routing. When blocked, route to competence, not hierarchy.

The one-sentence decision. A single sentence that captures the decision.

Digital records with AI-generated DAG. A technological elaboration on an ancient principle.

Tiered autonomy. The more triads that agree, the more autonomous the decision.

Faithfulness data collection. Tracking attendance, commitment, and facilitator volunteering.

Discrepancies

The 2-of-3 threshold for all decisions. The Bible has higher bars for serious matters. The tiered autonomy system addresses this.

The rejection clause is softer than Matthew 18:17. The protocol is more inclusive — refusal is data, visible to all.

The amendment window vs. covenant permanence. The Bible has permanent covenants. The protocol has revisitable decisions.

The protocol is secular. The Bible assumes a theological framework. The protocol can stand without God, but it shouldn't.

Challenges

1. The secular nature of the protocol. The protocol's accountability is to the record, not to God.

2. Tier thresholds are defaults, not absolutes. The CEO decides.

3. The rejection clause relies on human judgment.

4. The amendment window vs. covenant permanence.

5. Faithfulness data collection is new. How this data is interpreted and used is Phase II design.

Advanced Options

These are for when the simple path doesn't resolve. Most meetings won't need them.

Facilitator cannot be agreed

1. Vote. Each person names one facilitator. You cannot vote for yourself.

2. The person with the most votes facilitates.

3. If there is a tie, draw lots.

Neutral votes — detail

A neutral vote means: I do not have a position on this condition.

It is not "maybe." It is not "I abstain." It is: this condition is not one I can judge on the information I have, or it is not relevant to my part in this decision.

A neutral vote does not count toward the two-of-three. A condition needs at least two non-neutral votes to be resolved.
