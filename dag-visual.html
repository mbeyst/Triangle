Triangle Protocol — The DAG

How multiple decisions route through triads when the agenda is not a list

How it came about

The accident

I was describing an encryption technology. An AI misheard me and thought I was describing the protocol. But the structure it produced — a directed acyclic graph with threshold-based nodes, lateral routing, and immutable records — turned out to be exactly what the protocol needed.

The mishearing wasn't a failure. It was a productive collision. The encryption DAG and the protocol DAG share the same shape, and that isomorphism strengthens both.

Why it matters

The protocol currently achieves certain properties by convention — the record stays with the decision, the original stands marked as revisited, dissent is recorded. The encryption framing gives those properties a structural basis. They're not just good practice; they're what the structure guarantees.

The protocol works regardless of whether users know cryptography. The encryption layer is the architecture, not the user interface.

What the encryption layer strengthens

Tamper-evidence

The protocol says: "the original stands, marked as revisited, with the reasoning and the correction." That's currently a convention — people agree not to overwrite.

The encryption framing makes it structural: each decision node is hashed into the next. Any revision that overwrites rather than appends breaks the chain. The record cannot be silently altered. The protocol's accountability mechanism becomes a cryptographic guarantee.

This is what the "the record stays with the decision" line actually means — not a promise, a property.

Non-repudiation

The protocol records who voted what and why. That's currently a procedural requirement — people are asked to be honest.

The encryption framing makes each node authenticated by its author. You can't later claim you voted differently. The dissent record isn't just documentation — it's a signed statement. The competence tracking over time becomes verifiable, not just remembered.

Forward secrecy

The amendment window lets triad members change their mind within a closure period. After that, the decision stands.

The encryption framing makes this a structural property: new keys derive from old ones, but compromise of a later key doesn't expose earlier ones. The window isn't just a courtesy — it's a cryptographic boundary. Once closed, the decision is independent of the deliberation that produced it.

Threshold trust

Two of three is the minimal structure that treats every voice the same and both sides of a question the same. A supermajority privileges the status quo. Unanimity lets one person hold the group.

The encryption framing makes this a threshold scheme: no single participant can decrypt the message. No single triad member can force a decision. The 2-of-3 structure isn't just fair — it's the minimal threshold that prevents unilateral control while still allowing movement.

The closing review — and why the encryption layer helps

The problem

The protocol says: "Any decision spawning children should retain a closing decision reviewing the whole. This is specified and not yet implemented."

Without the closing review, the DAG is just decomposition — you break a big decision into small ones and never reconnect them. The children resolve, but the parent never reconvenes to integrate them.

How the encryption layer helps

In a Merkle DAG, the parent node's hash incorporates the hashes of its children. The parent literally cannot be finalised until the children are. The closing review isn't an optional step — it's the convergence point that the structure demands.

The children's outcomes become conditions for the parent. The triad votes again on D1, now informed by everything below it. The hash chain enforces the dependency: D1's record can't be sealed until D2, D3, D4 are sealed and their hashes incorporated.

This turns "specified, not implemented" into "specified by the structure." The closing review isn't a nice-to-have — it's what makes the DAG coherent.

Revisit alerts — error propagation

The protocol's mechanism

"If a parent is re-decided, revisit alerts flag descendants — so the error is seen not just at its source but everywhere it landed."

This is the accountability mechanism that makes the DAG more than a routing diagram. When D1 is flagged as wrong and reopened, D2, D3, D4 need to know — because they were decided in the context of a parent that may no longer hold.

The encryption framing

In a hash chain, a parent revision invalidates every descendant's hash. The structure itself propagates the alert — not as a notification, but as a broken link. Any descendant that doesn't re-hash against the revised parent is visibly stale.

This is stronger than a procedural alert. It's structural invalidation. The error isn't just communicated — it's mathematically detectable.

How the DAG fits the rest of the protocol

The triad is the atom. The DAG is the molecule.

Every node in the DAG runs the same surface → frame → vote → record flow. The DAG doesn't change any of the triad mechanics — two of three, per-condition voting, neutral votes, dissent recording, amendment window, outcome tracking. It just connects multiple triad decisions across time and ownership.

"The meeting is not the container. The decision is the container."

The DAG extends this. The DAG is the container for multiple decisions. Meetings are just the moments when triads convene to process nodes. A single meeting might process D1's framing, while D2, D3, D4 are being processed by their own triads in parallel — possibly in different meetings, possibly asynchronously.

The protocol works without cryptography

The encryption layer is the architecture, not the user interface. Users don't need to know about hash chains or threshold schemes. They vote, record dissent, track outcomes. The structural guarantees are there regardless — tamper-evidence, non-repudiation, forward secrecy — whether or not anyone thinks about them as cryptography.

The AI mishearing encryption as protocol wasn't a bug. It was the structure revealing itself through the wrong lens — and the lens turned out to be right.
