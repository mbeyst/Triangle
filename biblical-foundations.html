Biblical foundations

Sunday truth. Monday decisions.

Many Christian professionals know the split. On Sunday, they hear that every person bears God’s image, truth matters and power is accountable. On Monday, they enter a room where the highest title speaks first, agreement is safer than honesty, and the reasoning behind a decision disappears.

Triangle exists in that gap. It does not turn Scripture into a management manual. It asks a more modest question: what does the Bible repeatedly show about human authority, witness, work, truth and the keeping of a record — and what might that require of the organisations Christians help lead?

The gap is real

The church is not meant to produce one kind of person for worship and another for work.

A professional can sing about humility on Sunday, then be rewarded for never challenging a senior colleague on Monday. They can hear that people are not disposable, then sit in a meeting where the people closest to the consequence are absent. They can believe that truth sets people free, then work inside decisions whose assumptions, objections and reversals are quietly lost.

This is not mainly a problem of individual courage. It is a problem of architecture. A good person inside a structure that rewards silence will eventually learn silence. Triangle is an attempt to give truth, relevant knowledge and visible responsibility a place in the way consequential decisions are made.

The working claim

Faith should change more than a professional’s private ethics. It should change the shape of the room where consequential decisions are made.

The strongest points

Four foundations for the Triangle architecture

The claim is not that the Bible supplies a ready-made operating manual. It is that it repeatedly refuses hidden, isolated and unremembered power.

1. One clear proposition · Genesis 6:14–22 [2]

Consequential action begins with something clear enough to be tested.

Noah is not told to build something that floats. He is given a purpose, dimensions, materials, layout and a defined act of obedience. The narrative makes the instruction visible enough for compliance or refusal to mean something.

Triangle connection: a decision should start as one sentence someone can genuinely agree with or reject. Clarity is not bureaucratic debris. It is what lets people prepare, object, act and later learn from what was actually decided.

Limit: Genesis does not require businesses to mimic a divine specification. It does show that vague purpose is a poor foundation for consequential action.

2. The right three people · Exodus 18:13–27; Deuteronomy 19:15 [3] [4]

Authority does not mean carrying every decision alone.

Jethro sees Moses becoming the bottleneck for an entire people and names the problem plainly: the work is too heavy for one person. His answer is not a bigger meeting. It is the deliberate selection of capable, trustworthy people who reject dishonest gain. The law also refuses the sufficiency of a single witness in consequential accusation.

Triangle connection: the right people are chosen for relevant knowledge, character and consequence — not rank, volume or availability. Three is Triangle’s practical number: large enough for a minority position to exist, small enough for responsibility not to evaporate.

Limit: neither passage ordains a three-person corporate rule. The biblical pattern is plural, qualified witness and delegated responsibility.

3. A visible position · Numbers 13–14; 1 Kings 22 [5] [6]

A majority can decide and still be wrong.

Twelve spies see the same land and return with two incompatible judgements. Ten carry the fearful conclusion; Joshua and Caleb dissent. Later, Ahab receives the approval of hundreds of prophets, while Micaiah gives the warning no one wants to hear. In both accounts, numerical agreement does not certify truth.

Triangle connection: “The majority decides. The minority survives.” A minority view should not be softened into meeting minutes or lost under the pressure to sound united. Triangle gives it a named, visible place beside the final decision.

Limit: these stories do not teach anonymous polling. They expose the danger of treating confidence, seniority or numbers as a substitute for honest judgement.

4. A decision that remembers · Genesis 31:44–53; Acts 15:22–35 [7] [8]

Serious agreements need a durable, shared witness.

Jacob and Laban mark their covenant with a visible boundary and call it a witness. The Jerusalem Council reaches a decision, puts it in writing and sends the letter with trusted people to the churches affected by it. In both cases, the decision is not left inside the memories of the people who happened to be in the room.

Triangle connection: a consequential decision needs to keep its proposition, participants, positions, result, assumptions and distribution list. Otherwise the person with the most power can later rewrite what was known, disputed or promised.

Limit: a stone heap and an apostolic letter are not templates for a digital system. They show why a shared witness is not an administrative afterthought.

The necessary guardrail

Triangle is not a way to baptise hierarchy. It is a way to make human authority more answerable to truth.

In Genesis, Abraham questions the justice of what is about to happen to Sodom; the question is not treated as disloyalty. Joseph, holding immense political power over his brothers, refuses to take the place of God.[9] [10]

That matters because a decision architecture can be used badly. It can become another mechanism for silencing people while pretending to hear them. The biblical challenge is sharper: authority is real, but it is not absolute; it must be willing to hear what it does not want to hear and to leave an honest record behind.

The strongest claim we can make: Scripture does not prove that God ordained a two-out-of-three rule. It does repeatedly point toward qualified counsel, truthful witness, visible accountability, limits on power and remembered agreements. That is a serious foundation for asking better questions about the way Christians lead and decide.
